Product spec · draft v1 iOS August 2026

Ledger

A household's medical billing is not a stack of documents to analyze. It's a continuous ledger with an adversarial counterparty and statutory deadlines. Nobody has built it that way.

Thesis

Every existing consumer app in this space treats a medical bill as a document to analyze. Photograph it, get an LLM opinion, get a letter, delete the app. That's a one-shot tool in a category where the underlying problem recurs every time anyone in the household sees a doctor.

Ledger's promise is different: nothing gets paid that shouldn't, nothing gets missed that was owed to you, and no appeal window closes silently.

The v1 wedge needs no AI at all

Compare what the insurer's EOB says you owe against what the provider actually billed you. When those two numbers disagree, the provider is usually wrong — and the patient almost never checks, because the two documents arrive weeks apart in different envelopes.

Why now — the timing is the whole bet

CMS-0057-F requires impacted payers — Medicare Advantage organizations, state Medicaid and CHIP FFS, Medicaid/CHIP managed care, and QHPs on the federal exchanges — to operate four FHIR APIs by January 1, 2027. Operational prior-auth compliance dates began January 1, 2026, with first metrics reported by March 31, 2026. Public reporting is already live.

APIWhat it gives a consumer app
Patient AccessClaims, encounters, and — newly required under this rule — prior authorization data (excluding drugs)
Prior AuthorizationStatus and decision data on the exact events that generate denials
Payer-to-PayerContinuity when the user changes plans
Provider AccessNot consumer-facing

The hardest part of this product — getting structured, authoritative claim and denial data out of insurers — stops being a business-development problem and becomes a legal obligation on the counterparty, on a known date. An app architected through H2 2026 walks into a data supply that arrives on schedule. The incumbents shipping photo scanners are not building for it.

The window has an expiration

Roughly now through mid-2027. After that the data is commodity and the advantage shifts entirely to whoever accumulated the outcomes corpus first.

The user

Primary — the household administrator. One person per family absorbs all medical admin. Skews female, 35–60, often managing care for a child or parent as well as themselves. Financially literate; not clinically or legally trained. Trigger event: a bill arrives larger than expected, or a claim is denied.

  • “Do I really owe this?” — the reconciliation job. Highest frequency, lowest emotional stakes, best wedge.
  • “This was denied and I think it shouldn't have been.” — the appeal job. Lower frequency, highest stakes, monetizes hardest.
  • “Am I on track against my deductible?” — the position job. The reason to open the app when nothing is wrong.
  • “What's the deadline, and did I miss it?” — the anxiety job. This is the retention engine.

Explicit non-user: the uninsured, and anyone whose problem is affording a correct bill. That's charity care and financial-assistance screening — an adjacent, worthy, differently-shaped product.

The plan document is the differentiator

A generic model cannot tell you whether your plan covers a service. Your Summary of Benefits and Coverage can. On onboarding, extract deductible, out-of-pocket max, coinsurance, copay tiers, the preventive schedule, network tiers, and — critically — plan type, which determines the entire appeal regime. Every finding is then argued in the plan's own language, quoting the section. That's the difference between a letter that gets processed and one that gets filed.

Detection rules, v1

Deterministic first. Every rule below is arithmetic or a lookup, not a judgment call — high precision, no hallucinated accusations. Ship these before any model-based detection.

#RuleWhy it wins
R1Bill exceeds EOB patient responsibilityThe highest-frequency, highest-confidence error in American healthcare. Pure arithmetic.
R2Preventive billed as diagnosticACA mandates $0 cost-share for in-network preventive care. Rampant with screening colonoscopies and annual physicals.
R3Balance billing violation (No Surprises Act)Federal violation. The letter writes itself.
R4Timely-filing denial passed to patientCARC 29 present with patient responsibility > 0. The provider's failure to file is a provider write-off — the patient owes nothing.
R5Duplicate lineSame CPT + DOS + NPI + units, billed twice.
R6Deductible already satisfiedRequires the ledger. A single-document scanner structurally cannot catch this.
R7Out-of-pocket max exceededSame — this is the ledger's proof of value.
R8Cost-share exceeds plan termsCross-checks the EOB against the SBC.
R9In-network facility, out-of-network rate appliedCommon EOB processing error.
R10Billed before EOB processedDon't pay yet. Pure timing advice — builds trust cheaply.
R11Unbundling (NCCI edit pairs)Needs the NCCI tables; defer to v1.5 if it slips.
Two things that bite late

Model-assisted detection comes after the deterministic set is calibrated. The failure mode to avoid is an app that accuses a provider of fraud because a language model pattern-matched. Every finding shown to a user carries a dollar figure and a citation.

CARC and RARC code lists carry use licenses from X12 / WPC. Resolve licensing before shipping the lists in the binary — cheap to fix now, expensive to discover at review.

Deadline engine

A per-regime state machine, because the regimes genuinely differ and a missed deadline is the most common reason a winnable appeal is lost. Deadlines are deterministic, conservative — always show the earliest applicable date — and never model-generated.

Reminders escalate as the window closes — T-90 informational, T-30 actionable, T-14 urgent, T-7 daily, T-2 final. This is the notification stream that makes the app worth keeping installed, and it's honest: every alert is tied to a real statutory clock and a specific dollar amount.

The moat: the outcomes corpus

Every appeal records its result, keyed to (payer, plan_type, CARC/RARC, service category, argument). After a few thousand appeals the product answers a question no competitor and no general-purpose model can:

The question the corpus answers

“For this insurer, on this denial code, which argument actually gets overturned — and how often?” That routes users to the argument that wins rather than the one that sounds good.

It compounds with usage, can't be scraped, is a direct output of the core loop rather than a side project, and improves the headline metric rather than a vanity one. Design consequence: outcome capture is a first-class flow, not analytics. Prompt for it, one tap, and show the user how their answer improved the recommendation for the next person.

The paywall sits where the value is proven, not before it. The user sees “You're being overcharged $340” for free; the letter that recovers it is the purchase. Conversion should be strong because the ROI is arithmetic and already on screen.

Apple mechanics that materially change the model

Subscriptions are digital goods — IAP, 30% (15% under the Small Business Program). But Appeal Assist is a real-world service consumed outside the app: printing, certified mail, filing. Under guideline 3.1.3/3.1.5 it must not use IAP, and carries 0% Apple commission. Take it through Stripe. Post-Epic (2025), US-storefront apps may also link out to external purchase methods with no entitlement required — design for that deliberately rather than stumbling into a rejection.

Annual pricing matters: the underlying problem is annual — plan year, deductible reset — and annual plans blunt the 30%-faster-churn pattern that defines AI apps.

Open questions that gate the build

Q1 — Payer API unit economics · can kill v2

Reported historical aggregator pricing has run roughly $200–450 per month per connection at enterprise scope. Against a $29/mo consumer price that is catastrophically upside-down. Get written consumer-scale pricing from Flexpa (their annual platform model may price very differently) and 1upHealth before committing. Design around it if needed: periodic sync rather than continuous, connection as a premium tier, or one-time annual reconciliation. v1 does not depend on this.

  • Q2 — OCR accuracy on real EOBs. Every payer's layout differs and many are hostile to parsing. Measure extraction accuracy per field across 100 real EOBs from the top 10 payers. Below ~95% on patient_responsibility and confirm-and-correct becomes a primary flow, not an edge case.
  • Q3 — Does R1 actually fire often enough? The entire v1 wedge assumes bill-exceeds-EOB is common. Validate against real documents before build, not after.
  • Q4 — X12 licensing for the CARC/RARC code lists.

Compliance posture

  • Not a HIPAA covered entity as a direct-to-consumer tool — but the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule explicitly covers health apps, with enforcement precedent against GoodRx and Premom. Notice without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days; 500+ affected means notifying the FTC concurrently. Build the runbook before launch, not after an incident.
  • State health-privacy law — Washington's My Health My Data Act carries a private right of action and is the binding constraint. Have counsel confirm the current multi-state picture.
  • No legal or medical advice. The app prepares documents and surfaces the user's own plan terms and public regulations. Language discipline throughout: “Your plan's section 4.2 states…”, never “you should sue” or “this is medically necessary.”
  • No accusations of fraud. “Billed amount exceeds your EOB's patient responsibility by $340” — never “your provider is overbilling you.”
  • Data minimization as product. Retain documents only while the claim is open plus the appeal window; one-tap household deletion. This is also the marketing.
